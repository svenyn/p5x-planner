Scoring settings

Crit rate your supports add to every damage dealer: about 15 for one crit buffer such as Summer Ichigo, plus 5-10 from your Elucidator. At 0, only each character's own counts. Lufel's Crit Multiplier picks assume a crit support, so at 0 the tool reads crit rate as scarce and favours Crit Rate instead. It changes every build score, recommendation and Triage score; stat totals and stat targets stay each character's own.

Thieves Den in battle

What the Thieves Den — its level's items and its Qualia rating — adds to every character in combat. The game shows it on the stat screen once a battle starts, not on the character screen, so the difference between the two is the number to enter. It counts toward stat targets and the in-battle totals shown beside them, as lufel's targets include it; nothing else.

Cards by set

Keep or feed

Every unequipped card is scored against every character you own and rated by its best match, graded against the best card of the same slot.

70% of the best card in its slot and up is keep, 35% and up hold, below that feed. A card the Vault rates in the top 2% of its slot for some role reads lock instead of feed, and one it rates in the top 7% keeps its feed verdict with a note and sorts after the rest. A card the Upgrades tab recommends equipping is kept whatever its own score, because most of a set switch is the 4-piece bonus, which lands only with the fourth card.

The Score is the build-score points equipping the card adds for whoever gains most from it, against what they really wear, with the card's main stat priced at its level cap. It only knows the characters you have marked as owned — the Lock for later view is the counterweight.

Lock these

Cards worth locking in the game client because they will still be good later, whoever you own.

Keep or feed only knows the characters you own today, so it will mark a card feed when nobody on your current roster can wear it. This view is the counterweight. Two reasons qualify a card: it is loaded with the stats every damage dealer wants, or it is near the top of your inventory for one of the eight archetypes, which is the whole space of stat profiles the game asks for.

Rankings are within a slot and take no notice of who you own, so pulling a character cannot change them. Lock is the top 2% of a slot for its best role, hold the top 7%.

Better cards available

Every owned character's current cards against the best whole loadout your inventory can give them, showing only the slots that would change.

This reuses the Optimise tab's optimiser, so it agrees with what you would get running that tab per character; it just does all of them at once. With "Ignore cards equipped on someone else" unticked it also proposes cards worn by another character, tagged with their current owner and what taking the card costs them.

The number in brackets after each card is out of 100 for a perfect card in that slot for that character. Gain is what that one swap adds to the character's build score with their other slots left as they are, in build points, the same scale for everyone, which is also how characters are ordered. Rows that make up one 4-piece switch are bracketed with what the whole switch is worth; that is the number to read, since most of it is the set bonus, which lands only once all of them are equipped.

Weakest slots

One row per slot on every character you own, ranked by the build-score points a perfect card there would add.

This is "where is this character weakest", measured against a card that may not exist in your inventory, not "what can I equip today"; the other view answers that. Build + is the build-score points a perfect card in the slot would add, and compares across characters; the table is sorted by it out of the box.

Headroom is the raw score a perfect card for that slot would add over the one worn now, the same number shown beside every card score elsewhere in the tool. It is in one character's own score units, so it compares that character's five slots against each other and nothing else.

Custom card

Which characters gain most from a card you design in an event, and which card to make for each.

The Cycle of Fate event lets you choose a card's set, slot (Sun, Moon, Star or Sky), main stat and four substats; other events make a Space card the same way, so Card type switches between the two. For a Space card the set matters most: it only works with a 4-piece it pairs with, and the other Space sets that would also work are listed beside it. The score counts every Space set that pairs as equal (it does not weigh their different pairing effects), so the character's own recommended Space set comes first. You cannot choose the substats' values: each is a random roll, and the card's five upgrades land on random substats like any other card's. So every card here is valued at level 25 with the rolls you pick below. Typical is each substat at its average roll with the upgrades spread evenly, Perfect every roll at its maximum, Poor every roll at its lowest.

Gain is the build-score points the card adds over the best loadout your inventory can already give that character. Better cards you already own are counted as worn, so the gain is the new card's alone, and it compares across characters. Now is the build score of what they wear, Without the best they could wear today, With the best with the new card.

The card arrives at level 0, so how it turns out is luck: its starting values, which substats the five upgrades land on, and how big each one is. Likely is where the gain lands in 8 of 10 random levellings of the recommended card (hover for the average, worst and best seen). Gain uses the Rolls setting; with Typical it sits close to the middle of that range.

A new card can make other changes worth it, most often by completing a 4-piece you are one card short of. When it does, the row lists the other cards to equip with it. Substats are listed most valuable first; with perfect rolls the first one carries the extra upgrade.

Check a card

Enter the card as the event shows it — the substats' values are the level-0 rolls — to see what it is worth once levelled, the chance it ends up better than a fresh roll of the recommended card, and who else could use it. Useful for deciding whether to re-roll.

Ink card

What to make with Vicissitude Ink, how many substats are worth the Elysian Ink, and which substat to re-roll with Prophetic Ink.

The Customize button on the revelation screen makes a new 5★ card at level 0. One Vicissitude Ink buys a Sun, Moon, Star or Sky card in the set and with the main stat you choose. Elysian Ink picks substats: 1, 2, 4 or 8 of it in all for one, two, three or four of them. The game fills the rest of the card's four at random. Prophetic Ink rolls each picked substat's value, one each. One more re-rolls a picked substat, and the new value replaces the old one even when it is lower.

Gain is the build-score points the card adds over the best loadout your inventory can already give that character, averaged over what luck decides: the substats you did not pick and every value. It compares across characters. By substats picked shows the gain with none to all four picked, so you can see what each extra pick is worth against the Elysian Ink it costs. Likely is where the gain lands in 8 of 10 cards made and levelled at random.

Once the card is made, Check a roll below takes its substats and values and says which picked substat is worth a re-roll, if any.

Check a roll

Enter the card as the Customize screen shows it: all four substats with their level-0 values, the ones you picked ticked. It says what the card adds once levelled and which picked substat, if any, is worth a Prophetic Ink re-roll.

Fortune Revelation

Which set to choose when you redeem a Fortune Revelation (or an Orange one, for a Space card), and who each set would help.

You choose the set; the game rolls everything else: the slot (Sun, Moon, Star or Sky — always Space for an Orange Fortune Revelation), the main stat, which three or four substats the card starts with, their values, and where its five upgrades land. So no set guarantees a good card. This view draws the same 400 random cards in every set, levels each to 25, and checks what each one would add to every character you count, over the best loadout your inventory already gives them.

A card can only be worn by one character, so each draw is worth what it adds to whoever gains most from it. Avg value is that, averaged over all the draws, in build points (the same 0-100 scale as the build score); a draw that helps nobody counts as zero, which is why the numbers are small. A set that is often a modest upgrade for one character can beat one that is occasionally a big one for several. Useful is how often a draw is an upgrade for anyone at all.

Open a row to see who the set helps: how often the card would go to them, how often it is an upgrade for them at all, what it adds when it is, and which slot and main stat rolls do it. A card can also complete a 4-piece someone is one card short of; that is counted. Sets marked about as good are too close to the best to separate on luck alone.

No source publishes the drop odds, so they are taken from your own inventory: main stats about evenly spread within a slot, 4 in 10 new cards with four substats, every substat equally likely. Unreleased sets are left out.

Retrograde Card

Which levelled card gains most from a reset: good substats for its character, upgrades that went elsewhere.

A Retrograde Card sets a card back to level 0. The substats it dropped with keep their starting values; the upgrades are undone, and levelling it to 25 again lands five new ones on random substats. Each card here is reset and levelled again 300 times at random, and every outcome is priced for the character beside it.

Nothing records where a card's upgrades went, so they are read from its values: a substat holding three rolls can only show a value between three of its lowest and three of its highest rolls. ×3 beside a substat means its starting roll plus two upgrades (×~3 when the values leave it unclear). A card is worth resetting when the substats holding many rolls are the faded ones, the stats its character does not want.

A card that dropped with three substats got its fourth at its first upgrade, so a reset takes that one off too. The values also tell which cards those are (Dropped with). Whether the fourth comes back the same is not known: Prydwen's guide says a Retrograde Card can change it, so by default it comes back as a random stat. Switch Fourth substat if you find out otherwise.

For is the wearer, or for a spare card the character in scope it would help most. Gain is the build-score points a reset adds on average against keeping the card, compared across characters. A spare card is never counted below what that character would wear without it, so a reset that still loses to their own card adds nothing; a worn card that comes out worse costs what it costs, since the old card is gone. Likely is where 8 of 10 resets land, and Better / worse the share of resets that beat, or fall short of, keeping it. Only 5★ cards are rated: the roll values are measured for 5★ only.

Save your work

This tool keeps nothing between sessions on its own — download a save file when you are done, and load it back next time. Sync with GitHub

Keep the save in a private GitHub repository, so the laptop and the phone share one. Once connected, the header's Load and Save use it instead of a file.

The buttons above still download and load files. Every save to GitHub is kept there as a version, so an older one can always be fetched from the repository's history. If the save on GitHub was changed on another device since this one loaded it, Save asks before replacing it.

Setting it up, once:

  1. On GitHub, create a new repository, e.g. p5x-planner-save, and set it to Private. It can stay empty.
  2. Make an access token: your picture → Settings → Developer settings → Personal access tokens → Fine-grained tokens → Generate new token. Pick an expiry; under Repository access choose Only select repositories and that repository; under Permissions → Repository permissions set Contents to Read and write. Generate it and copy it.
  3. On each device, fill in the repository and the token below and press Connect.

The token can read and write that one repository and nothing else. "Remember on this device" keeps the repository name and the token in this browser so the app opens on your save; your cards are never kept there. Untick it on a shared computer. If a device is lost, delete the token on GitHub.

Where this data came from, and what to distrust

Sets, Space cards, character builds and base stats come from lufel.net, regenerated on 25 Sep 2026 under the Global client's names. Main stat values and substat roll tiers were measured from your own scanned 5★ cards. Four things are worth knowing before you trust a recommendation:

  1. Stat totals are floors. My Palace and party buffs are not counted, so you may already clear a target this says you miss. The one party buff you can set is the team crit rate, from the header.
  2. Only 5★ main stat values are measured. Other rarities are assumed to follow the same curve, and a lower-rarity card with no scanned main value is treated as unknown, never as zero.
  3. Release status is lufel's. Its tags can lag the Global client by a patch. A set or Space card marked unreleased stays in the tables so imports still resolve, but is never recommended; if you can already farm one, the tags are behind the game.
  4. Weights are lufel's order, not lufel's numbers. Lufel ranks each character's substats; turning that ranking into weights is this tool's own judgement, and a few characters carry recorded exceptions.

All of it gets better the moment it is checked against your own game: a screenshot that disagrees with a number here is worth more than any change to the scoring.

Scanning your cards in

Typing four thousand cards into the Inventory tab is not a plan. The ocr/ folder in this repository reads card detail panels straight off the screen while you click through the card list, and writes the JSON file the Import scan button imports. The install, the calibration and the one-off check are done once; after that a scan is a single command.

It reads pixels. Nothing here reads the game's memory or talks to a server; it looks at the screen and nothing else. One file is the exception — autowalk.py sends the WASD keypresses that move you through the list, so you do not have to. That is opt-in, lives in its own file, and every other tool here stays strictly read-only. Sending input to a game client is a ban risk on an account worth thousands of cards; it is on the table only because you decided it was, and it is easy to leave alone — watch.py does the same job while you tap the keys yourself.

Install the tooling once

You need Python 3.10+ and Tesseract OCR on your PATH. Then, from the repository root: 

pip install -r ocr/requirements.txt
npm run export-tables

That second command writes dist/tables.json: every slot, set, substat, alias and character name exported straight out of src/data.js. The pipeline matches OCR text against that file rather than against a hand-copied list, so it cannot drift from the app it is feeding. Re-run it after any edit to src/data.js.

Calibrate the crop regions once per device

The pipeline has to be shown where each field sits on your screen. Point it at a single real screenshot: 

python ocr/calibrate.py path/to/one-screenshot.png

A window opens on that image, once per field. Drag a box around the field and press Enter or Space to confirm; press Enter without dragging to skip a field this particular card does not have, such as the fourth substat on a three-substat card; Esc quits without writing anything. What to aim at:

FieldAim at
slot_tabsThe whole Space/Sun/Moon/Star/Sky tab strip, top-left. Read by colour, not text, so the box only has to contain all five tabs.
titleThe bracketed set name inside the Set Effect box, lower right — e.g. [Futility]. Not the big stylized title at the top: that jagged comic-book font could not be read under any Tesseract configuration tried against a real screenshot. The same name appears in the Set Effect box in a plain readable font, which is why it is read from there.
levelThe Lv.25 text, tight around it.
main_labelThe name of the first stat — the one with its own icon, e.g. Crit Rate — not its number. Sun and Space cards have a fixed main stat, so this is not used for them.
sub1…sub4A label box and a value box for each substat row. The value box matters more than it looks: ATK, DEF and HP each exist as both a flat and a percentage substat under the same label, and only the % in the value tells them apart.

The result lands in ocr/config/crop_regions.json as fractions of the image, so it is resolution-independent and only has to be done once per device. To redo a single bad box without dragging all twelve again: 

python ocr/calibrate.py path/to/one-screenshot.png --only sub4_label sub4_value
Moving the level box invalidates the level templates. The level is matched against known renders rather than read (see the review step below), and those renders are tied to that box at that pixel size. Recalibrate it and they stop matching — safely, they refuse rather than guess, but every card lands in review until you rebuild them: python ocr/build_level_templates.py --shots <your screenshots folder>.

Check it can see the game once, and after any change

Open the inventory with a card selected, run this, and alt-tab back to the game during the five-second countdown: 

python ocr/watch.py --probe --rarity 5

It waits for the panel to hold still, grabs one frame, and tells you what it found: the resolution, which slot tab is lit, and the card it read. Every setup problem shows up here, and each looks different:

What it saysWhat is wrong
The frame is entirely blackThe game is in fullscreen exclusive mode, which a screen grab cannot see through. Switch it to borderless windowed.
Wrong resolutionThe crop boxes are fractions of the frame, so they only land correctly at the resolution you calibrated on. If you play windowed, add --window "Persona5" to grab the game's client area instead of the whole screen.
No lit tab foundThe inventory is not open, or the slot_tabs box needs recalibrating.
Never held stillSomething is animating continuously. Anything read is suspect — a mid-animation artefact and a bad crop box look identical and have completely different fixes.
Fields cut off or emptyCrop boxes need moving. The frame is kept at ocr/_probe.png, so you can point calibrate.py straight at it.

A clean read is not the same as a correct one. Check the values against the panel with your own eyes before scanning hundreds of cards — the checks catch what is illegal, not what is merely wrong.

Capture the cards per scan

Start the watcher, then click through the card list. It reads each panel off the screen as you go — no screenshot key, no folder of images to move afterwards. 

python ocr/watch.py --rarity 5

It prints a line per card and stops on Ctrl-C, writing the same two files the pipeline writes. Two cards per row means the keyboard walk is a repeating D S A S cycle — one keypress per card. Run the check above first; it catches every setup problem in one command, and they all look like OCR faults if you skip it.

Or have it press the keys too. autowalk.py walks the list and reads it, so one command scans and exits: 

python ocr/autowalk.py --rarity 5 --tabs all --dry-run   # prints the plan, sends nothing
python ocr/autowalk.py --rarity 5 --tabs all             # every tab, to the end of each
python ocr/autowalk.py --rarity 5                        # just the tab that is open

Always --dry-run first. It counts down five seconds so you can alt-tab to the game, then locks onto whatever window is in front and checks that window is still in front before every key and click — so alt-tab is the stop gesture, and a stray D cannot land in your browser. Start it on any card: it presses W until nothing moves, reads which column the red selection frame is in, and presses A only if it needs to, so it begins at the first card. It tracks where the cursor is, so it knows the end of the list when it gets there — a whole cycle that turns up nothing new ends the tab — and --count is only a hard ceiling behind that. With --tabs all it then clicks the next tab on the strip, checks by colour that the strip really changed before it sends another key, and walks that one too. Set the game's rarity filter first and run once per rarity: that is the one thing the screen never shows.

This is the one thing that sends input to the game. It is a fixed repeating key cycle, which is exactly the shape of a macro, and that is a ban risk. There is deliberately no randomised timing or anything else that would help it avoid notice — accepting a risk is not the same as hiding from it. watch.py above does the identical job with your own fingers on the keys.
Let the panel settle. The stat labels animate into place when a card is selected, and a frame caught halfway through holds a real but part-assembled label — ntAcc. A for Ailment Acc. — with the crop box perfectly correct. The watcher only reads a panel that has held still for --settle seconds (0.25 by default), so it cannot capture a half-drawn label. If you outrun it the card goes past unread, it clicks at you, and it tells you how many you missed at the end. Tap a little slower, or lower --settle.

Three capture rules decide whether this works at all:

  1. One rarity per run. Rarity is not printed on the detail panel anywhere, so you supply it on the command line and every card in the run is stamped with it. Use the game's own rarity filter and scan your 5★ separately from your 4★. A mis-stamped 5★ run does get caught: every card is checked against what the scan itself proved — a level-0 substat must be one of its stat's five roll values and the main stat's value must be the one its level prints — so a card whose numbers are not a 5★'s goes to review instead of into the inventory.
  2. Slot comes free, so tabs can be mixed. It is read off the Space/Sun/Moon/Star/Sky tab strip by colour — which tab is highlighted red — because the game's title font is unreadable to OCR. That happens per frame, so switching tabs mid-run is fine. Only pass --slot when you are staying on one tab: it is a fallback for frames where the strip is hidden, and every card whose strip disagrees with it goes to review rather than being filed wrongly.
  3. Same resolution and window size throughout. Crop boxes are stored as fractions of the frame, so they survive between sessions — but not across a resolution change without recalibrating. Play fullscreen at the resolution you calibrated on.

A tooltip, popup or notification over a field reads as noise and sends that card to review. The watcher does at least know what screen you are on — no lit tab means the inventory is not up, so menus, loading screens and battles are skipped rather than captured as unreadable cards. You can leave it running.

Catching up after a few pulls. Save first (the header's Save button), sort the inventory by order obtained, and start from the newest end: 

python ocr/watch.py --rarity 5 --known p5x-save.json --expect 12

Each card is labelled new or already-held as it is read, and it stops once it has found the 12 new ones. Use --stop-after 20 instead if you do not know the number — it stops after twenty consecutive cards you already have. Neither is load-bearing: every card read is written out either way, and the import below is the authority on what is actually new. Getting the number wrong costs you a few keypresses, nothing more.

Or capture by hand. One screenshot per card, all in one folder — filenames do not matter; .png, .jpg and .jpeg are all read. Then point pipeline.py at the folder instead, as below. The same three rules apply, minus the settle guard, which is why a hand-taken batch is the one that produces scrambled labels.

Two things are never scanned because the panel does not show them: rarity, handled above, and which character the card is equipped on. You set that in the app instead: run the tab and press Mark these as equipped to record a whole loadout at once, or set Equipped on in a card's edit dialog to record one card — useful when what you actually have on is not what the optimiser recommends.

Run the pipeline only if you captured by hand

python ocr/pipeline.py path/to/screenshots --rarity 5 --slot Star --out cards.json

Skip this step if you used the watcher — it has already written both files. Either way, every card that comes out is checked against the same rules this app applies to a pasted line or a loaded save file — legal slot, legal set for that slot, legal main stat, substat count for the rarity, no substat duplicating the main, no value beyond what five maximum rolls could produce. Two files are written:

cards.jsonCards that read cleanly and passed validation. This is the file you import.
cards-review.jsonEverything else — each entry carrying the source frame's filename, the card exactly as it was read, a per-field confidence score, the validation errors and a plain-English note about what was ambiguous. Only written when something is actually flagged; a batch that comes back completely clean leaves no file behind.

A card that fails on one field still shows you every other field it read, so working the review pile is usually a one-number fix rather than a rescan. Try five screenshots before you try five hundred.

Import into the planner per scan

Press Import scan in the header (or → Import cards (merge)) and pick cards.json. You can select several at once, and you should: a session leaves one file per rarity tier, and importing them separately merges each against an inventory the previous import already changed. Picked together they are read into a single review. This merges rather than replaces: new cards are added, cards that have leveled up since your last scan are updated in place, exact resubmissions are skipped, and anything that looks like a card you already own but whose numbers do not line up as a clean level-up is parked in a review table where you choose skip, add as new or replace. Rescanning a slot you have already scanned is therefore safe.

Then press Download save file on that same tab. This app deliberately keeps nothing between sessions — no cookies, no browser storage — so an import you do not save is an import you lose when the tab closes.

Work the review pile per scan

No *-review.json file means nothing was flagged — its absence is the all-clear, not something to go check for. When one exists, look for a pattern before touching individual cards: if the same error appears across dozens of them, a crop box is wrong, not the cards. Recalibrate that one field with --only and re-run the batch rather than correcting each by hand.

The level is matched, not read. That small slanted Lv.0 is the one field OCR cannot do reliably — two captures of the same card differ by nine pixels, and that is enough to turn Lv.0 into 9. Since the game draws a given level identically every time, it is compared against known renders instead, which is exact. A level it has never seen comes back unknown and lands here rather than being guessed at — and correcting it below teaches that render, so the same level is only ever unknown once.

For what's left, walk the pile card by card instead of hand-editing JSON:

python ocr/review_cards.py cards   # reads cards-review.json / cards.json
python ocr/review_cards.py cards-2 # for a batch run with --out cards-2.json

Each card shows as a numbered field list with the note that flagged it. Type a field's number to correct it — values resolve through the same vocabulary the OCR itself uses, so crit dmg becomes Crit Multiplier and a typo is rejected with the legal options listed rather than written through unchecked. Then:

aAccept the card as shown. Refused while any X line is still showing, so fix those first. Accepting is what actually clears the card to merge — most of what lands in review is a value that is perfectly legal and simply wrong (a real Lv.18 misread as Lv.8 passes every range check there is), so a card that already looks right still gets held until you say so.
dDrop the card — the frame was bad, not the read. Delete the screenshot and recapture that card.
oOpen the source screenshot in your image viewer to check the panel directly. Needs --shots path/to/screenshots (searched recursively — point it at the screenshots root, no need to name the exact batch subfolder) or a P5X_SHOTS environment variable set the same way. Windows only.
qStop here. Everything not yet accepted or dropped — including a card you were mid-edit on — stays in the review file for next time.

Accepted cards merge into cards.json automatically once you finish the pile. Prefer editing the JSON in a text editor instead? Correct the card object by hand, clear its notes and errors arrays, then merge on its own: 

python ocr/merge_review.py cards-2

Either way, only entries with both arrays empty are merged, and every card is re-validated on the way in — so a correction typed wrong is held and printed with the reason rather than written through. Once every entry is resolved, the review file is deleted rather than left behind empty.

When it goes wrong

What you seeWhat it usually means, and what to do
Every card lands in review with could not resolve set from title The title box is on the big stylized card title, which OCR cannot read. Recalibrate it onto the bracketed name in the Set Effect box: --only title.
Levels come back as nonsense — Lv 128 on a 5★ The level box is catching neighbouring text or the enhancement bar. Tighten it with --only level. The validator catches this one for you, since 25 is the ceiling.
could not read the slot tab strip The strip is occluded or out of frame, or the frame is not a detail panel at all. Pass --slot so there is a fallback, and drop the frames that are not cards.
slot tab strip reads "Sky" but slot="Star" was passed in The capture mixed two slot tabs. What the strip says wins, because per-frame evidence beats a batch-wide assumption, but the card still goes to review rather than being silently resolved. Split the capture by tab.
"Ruin" is not a legal set for Sky Not your fault and not fixable by recalibrating. Two set names from the community guides — Ruin and Prudence — could not be matched to any verified set list. See the provenance notes on the tab.
A substat value is rejected as out of range That sub{n}_value box is overlapping the row above or below. Recalibrate the one box.
A set or stat name resolves to the wrong thing OCR text is matched exactly first, then by alias, and only then fuzzily above an 80% threshold — a fuzzy hit is recorded with its confidence in the review file. If a name is wrong across the board, the fix belongs in src/data.js, followed by npm run export-tables.

If you would rather not install any of that

Nothing here is required. The tab takes cards one at a time through a keyboard-driven form, and the tab's file picker takes a whole cards.json or saved file at once, merging it into what you already have. For a few dozen cards the form is faster than calibrating anything. It is the four-thousand-card case the pipeline exists for.
